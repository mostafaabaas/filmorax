Guide 1 · The brief

How to write a production brief for a commercial

A production brief is the document the whole shoot is priced, cast and scheduled from. A vague brief does not produce a vague film by accident — it produces an expensive one, because every missing answer becomes a decision made on the day, at day rates. A working brief has exactly these parts:

The communication job

  • One objective. Launch awareness, a product story, a seasonal push — one line. A film asked to do everything does none of it.
  • Who it is for, and where it will run. Audience, markets, and the final placements — TV, YouTube, reels, in-store screens. Placements decide formats, durations and how much dialogue survives without sound.
  • The single message. What a viewer should think or feel afterwards, in one sentence the team can test every scene against.

The product and the proof

What must be shown about the product — the demo moment, the packaging, the result — and the claims that may be said, worded the way legal allows them. If the product has to be somewhere specific (a store, a car, a kitchen), say so now; locations are scouted from the brief.

References, not templates

Two or three links to films whose tone you want — and one sentence on what to take from each: the pacing, the humour, the grade. References set direction; frame-by-frame copies kill the casting and the day.

Scope and deliverables

  • The deliverables list. The master and every cut-down: durations, sizes, languages, subtitles — listed, not assumed.
  • Days and locations. Your best understanding of how many shoot days and what kind of locations — a good production company will challenge it honestly rather than pad it.
  • Talent needs. Faces, ages, dialogue or mute performance, and any known casting sensitivities.
  • Deadline and approval chain. The date the film must be live, and exactly who signs off at treatment, casting and final cut — late approvals are the most common cause of rushed endings.

Guide 2 · The shoot day

What a one-day shoot actually involves

A shoot day is not "a camera and a location" — it is a temporary factory that exists for around twelve working hours, and everything it makes has to be made inside them. Here is what a single day actually contains, so you can judge any production plan — ours or anyone's:

Before the camera rolls

Crew and equipment arrive first: the location is dressed and lit, product and wardrobe are prepped, and the director blocks the first scene while makeup and sound get ready. This setup block is real time on the schedule — a "9-to-7 shoot" does not mean ten hours of filming.

Who is on set (a typical commercial crew)

  • Direction and production: the director, an assistant director running the clock, and a producer or production manager keeping the day legal, safe and fed.
  • Camera and light: the DOP, camera assistants, a gaffer and sparks for lighting, grip for movement and rigging.
  • Sound: a sound recordist — clean dialogue is captured on the day or paid for later, if it can be recovered at all.
  • Art and talent: the art department for set and props, wardrobe and makeup for the cast, the cast themselves — plus catering, because a crew that is not fed on schedule loses the afternoon.

The schedule runs backwards from the light

Scenes are ordered by location and daylight, not by script order. The call sheet — who is needed, where, at what time — is the law of the day, and a good assistant director protects the wrap time the way a pilot protects fuel: overtime exists, but it is a decision, not a drift.

What you get at wrap

Not a film. You get the raw material: camera cards backed up twice before anyone leaves, the sound files, and a day that either covered the shot list or did not. The film itself is built in post — edit, grade, sound design, titles, versions — which is why a serious quote separates shoot days from post days instead of bundling them into a single number.

Guide 3 · Rights

Video usage rights for ads, explained

Paying for a film's production is not the same as owning every use of it — forever, everywhere. This is the most common expensive surprise in commercial production, so here is how usage actually works:

Separate the film from the faces in it

Two different rights live inside one commercial: the production deliverable — the edited film itself — and the talent's image rights: the actors' and models' permission for their faces and voices to be used in advertising. The video file can sit on your drive forever; the faces inside it are licensed for agreed media and a period. When a campaign outlives the talent term, the film cannot legally keep running until that term is renewed.

What a usage agreement defines

  • Media. TV, digital and social, out-of-home, in-store screens, cinema — each is a separate line, not a bundle by default.
  • Territory. One country, the region, or worldwide.
  • Duration. The period the ad may run with those faces, starting from first air — and what renewal costs if the campaign continues.
  • Exclusivity. Whether the talent may appear in a competitor's ads during the term, and in which category exactly.

Music and stock have terms too

Library music and stock footage inside your film carry their own licences — media, territory and duration again. A track cleared for social is not automatically cleared for TV. Keep the licence documents with the masters; a platform or broadcaster dispute two years later is answered with paperwork, not memory.

Agree it before the shoot, in writing

Usage decided at casting is a line in a quote; usage renegotiated after a successful campaign is a negotiation where the talent knows exactly what their face is worth to you. Put media, territory, duration and exclusivity in the production agreement before anyone is cast — then the campaign can run as long as you planned it to.
